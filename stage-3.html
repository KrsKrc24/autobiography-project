Two different reactions to my questions

As a teenager, I continued living in Issaquah with my family. Asking questions was still one of my favorite ways to learn.

During a trip to Glacier National Park, I went whitewater rafting. I was interested in what the guide was saying and kept asking questions. After a while, I felt like he was getting frustrated.

The memory is funny to look back on, but it also made me think about how my questions affect other people.

Asking questions has also opened doors for me. I emailed Dr. Greenberg at Fred Hutch with questions about his lab’s research. I kept writing back and even asked why certain approaches would not work, and he took the time to explain. Those conversations turned into a research assistant position in his immunology lab, where I now help test ways to edit T cells, a type of immune cell. I was the first high school student the lab had taken on.

The rafting trip taught me that questions can wear people out. My time with Dr. Greenberg taught me that the right question, at the right time, can lead somewhere.

Connections to the textbook

1. Metacognition

Metacognition means thinking about your own thinking. It includes noticing how you learn and deciding whether your approach is working.

When I first started research at the Li Lab at WSU Spokane, I mostly followed the steps without really understanding why each one mattered. Later, I chose the drug I had studied in the lab for an AP Biology project. That project helped me connect my lab data to how the drug works. Once I understood the why, I got better at explaining my research too. This was metacognition because I noticed a gap in my own understanding and found a way to work on it. (Riser et al., 2024, Section 9.3)

2. Emotional intelligence

Emotional intelligence includes recognizing emotions in yourself and others and using that awareness to guide your behavior.

The rafting memory reminds me to notice how another person might be feeling. I am learning to balance my excitement with listening and giving other people space. My guess about the guide’s feelings could have been wrong, so checking is better than assuming. (Riser et al., 2024, Section 10.2)

My theme

At the Li Lab, connecting my research to an AP Biology project helped me move beyond following steps to understanding why they mattered. The rafting trip and my conversations with Dr. Greenberg also showed me that the way I ask questions matters.

These experiences connect to plasticity because I could adjust how I learned and communicated. My earlier interests gave me a starting point, and new people and experiences helped me build on it. (Bermudez, 2026)

The people and opportunities around me

My family and cultural traditions still shape what matters to me. At the same time, talking with teachers, classmates, and other adults has shown me that people have different ways of communicating.

Being able to travel gave me a chance to learn from a guide in a new place. That reminds me that opportunities to explore are shaped partly by the resources and experiences available to us.

Having a researcher take the time to answer my questions gave me an opportunity I could not have created alone. My effort mattered, and so did having access to a mentor.

What I see now

The rafting trip and my conversations with Dr. Greenberg taught me different things about asking questions. Questions can help me learn and open doors, but the timing and the other person’s needs matter. At the Li Lab, I also learned that following steps is different from understanding them. Now I want to ask questions that help me understand the work, while listening carefully to the answers.
